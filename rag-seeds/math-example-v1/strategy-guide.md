# Problem-solving strategy for grade-school math word problems

Grade-school word problems look harder than they are because they
hide the arithmetic inside sentences. A simple four-step process
works for most problems.

## Step 1 — Read carefully, twice

Read the problem through once to understand what's happening. Read
it again looking specifically for:

- **Numbers that appear** — write them down, with units
- **The question being asked** — what quantity does the problem
  want you to find?
- **Keywords that signal operations:**
  - *"total"*, *"altogether"*, *"combined"*, *"sum"* → addition
  - *"left"*, *"remain"*, *"difference"*, *"fewer"* → subtraction
  - *"per"*, *"each"*, *"times as many"*, *"groups of"* → multiplication
  - *"split"*, *"shared equally"*, *"how many groups"*, *"per group"* → division
  - *"half as many"*, *"twice as many"*, *"three times as much"* → multiplication by 1/2, 2, 3

## Step 2 — Identify what's given vs. what's asked

For every problem, split the information into:

- **Given quantities** (the data in the problem)
- **The unknown** (what you need to compute)
- **Relationships** (how the given quantities connect to the unknown)

Writing this down helps when the problem has three or more quantities.

## Step 3 — Set up an equation or sequence of steps

Translate the words into math:

- A single-step problem: one arithmetic operation produces the answer
- A multi-step problem: compute an intermediate quantity first,
  then use it to compute the final answer

**Show your work step by step.** This matters more than finding
the fastest solution — it lets you check each step independently.

## Step 4 — Check the answer makes sense

Before writing down your final answer, ask:

- **Units** — did the problem ask for apples and I computed apples?
- **Magnitude** — if the problem had small numbers, is my answer
  reasonable in size?
- **Direction** — if the problem said "how many more", is my
  answer positive (not negative or zero)?

A quick sanity check catches most arithmetic errors.

## Common error patterns

- **Confusing "how many more" with "how many total"** — one is a
  difference, the other is a sum.
- **Forgetting a step** — multi-step problems are the most common
  source of errors. Compute the intermediate quantity before you
  touch the final one.
- **Ignoring half-step quantities** — "Fri/Sat/Sun she works half
  days" means the daily number is halved for those three days,
  not that only three half-days exist in the week.
- **Unit errors** — hours vs. minutes, dollars vs. cents, miles
  vs. kilometers. Convert explicitly.
- **Rate × time direction** — if the problem gives a rate
  ("50 miles per hour") and a quantity ("in 3 hours"),
  multiply; don't divide.
